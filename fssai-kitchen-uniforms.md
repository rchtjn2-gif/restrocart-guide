# What FSSAI expects food handlers to wear, and how to turn it into a uniform spec

Food safety inspections in India look at people as well as fridges and floors. Whether hair is covered, whether coats are clean and whether the cook at the pass is wearing street shoes are among the most visible signs of a kitchen's hygiene. This page summarises what the rules say about clothing and how to turn them into an order list. It is practical guidance, not legal advice; check the current regulations or a food safety consultant for your licence.

## Where the rules come from

- The parent law is the **Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006**.
- The day-to-day hygiene requirements sit in **Schedule 4** of the **Food Safety and Standards (Licensing and Registration of Food Businesses) Regulations, 2011**. Part I covers general hygiene for registered food businesses; Part V covers licensed catering and food service establishments.
- FSSAI's basic food safety training manual for catering, used in its FoSTaC training for Food Safety Supervisors, summarises the personal hygiene rules.

The key clothing line is that food handlers should wear **suitable clean protective clothing, head covering, face mask, gloves and footwear**. The training material also says street shoes should not be worn while preparing food, visitors to the kitchen should wear protective clothing, and staff should have changing facilities.

## What the rule does not say

It does not prescribe a colour, fabric or style. A white double-breasted chef coat is not legally required. A clean, suitable garment that protects food from contamination is. That leaves room to design a kitchen uniform that matches your brand.

## Turning each requirement into an order

| Requirement | In practice | What to order |
|---|---|---|
| Clean protective clothing | A clean garment over personal clothes at the start of every shift | Chef coat or chef tee plus bib apron, about three sets per person |
| Head covering | All hair contained | Skull caps, beret caps or bandana caps; hair nets under caps for long hair; beard nets where needed |
| Face mask | Mouth and nose covered where the task needs it | Washable or disposable masks, changed regularly |
| Gloves | Hand protection for ready-to-eat food and hot work | Disposable food-grade gloves; heat-resistant gloves for tandoor and grill |
| Footwear | No street shoes while preparing food | Closed, non-slip kitchen shoes that stay at work |
| Visitors | Protection for anyone entering the kitchen | Spare aprons and disposable caps kept at the kitchen door |

## Why three sets per person

One set is worn, one is in the wash and one is spare for spills or double shifts. With fewer, someone ends up wearing yesterday's coat.

## Design details that help hygiene

- **Secure fastenings.** Loose buttons can fall into food; studs or cloth knot buttons are safer.
- **No chest pockets** on kitchen coats, so pens and phones cannot drop into a pan.
- **Full hair coverage** from the cap, not just a token band.
- **Uniforms stay in the building.** Staff change at work, which is also why changing space matters.

## Branding still works

Caps, aprons and coats can all carry embroidered names and logos while meeting the rule.
