# Embroidery, print, DTF or patch: branding restaurant uniforms the right way

A logo can go onto a uniform in at least six ways, and the method you pick decides three things: how the garment looks on the first day, how it looks after a few months of commercial washing, and what the order costs. The common mistake is choosing one method for the whole brand. The better approach is to choose per garment.

## The short version

- **Embroidery** wins for anything washed hot and often: chef coats, aprons, polos, caps.
- **Screen printing** wins for big, flat-colour graphics in volume, such as back prints and merchandise tees.
- **DTF (direct-to-film)** wins for small runs and full-colour or gradient artwork.
- **Sublimation** wins for all-over patterns on light polyester, such as dry-fit delivery tees.
- **Leather, leatherette or woven patches** win where you want a craft look or where the fabric is too heavy to stitch neatly.

## Method by method

### Embroidery

Thread is stitched into the fabric, so the logo has depth and survives hot washes, bleach exposure and years of wear. That is why it is the default for kitchen and service garments. The limits are physical: very fine detail (roughly below 4 mm) disappears, gradients cannot be stitched, every extra thread colour adds cost, and large stitched areas get stiff. If your logo has thin serifs or a photographic element, ask for a simplified crest drawn specifically for embroidery. It usually looks better at chest size than the original.

### Screen printing

Ink is pushed through a mesh and heat-cured. Each colour needs its own screen, so setup cost rises with the number of colours, but cost per piece barely moves after that. Prints soften and eventually crack with washing, so it suits garments you expect to replace about once a year.

### DTF and digital printing

No colour limit and no screens, which makes it the practical choice for short runs, merch drops and complex artwork. Durability sits between screen printing and embroidery.

### Sublimation

Dye becomes part of polyester fibre, so there is no feel and almost no wear. It only works on polyester and on light base colours.

### Patches

Made separately and stitched or riveted on. Woven patches hold finer detail than embroidery at small sizes. Debossed leather or metal-stamped patches suit breweries, steakhouses and specialty coffee, and they solve the problem of branding a heavy denim apron.

## A garment-by-garment cheat sheet

| Garment | Recommended method |
|---|---|
| Chef coat, shirt or polo chest crest | Embroidery |
| Cap front | Embroidery |
| Apron bib | Embroidery, or a leather patch on denim or canvas |
| Large back graphic | Screen print (volume) or DTF (small run) |
| Photographic or gradient artwork | DTF |
| All-over pattern on polyester | Sublimation |

## Before you approve the order

1. Send a vector logo (AI, EPS, SVG or PDF) rather than a JPEG.
2. Ask to see the logo rendered as a stitched crest, a print and a patch before you commit.
3. Check the thread or ink colour against your brand palette in daylight, not only on screen.
4. Confirm the logo size in centimetres for each garment.
